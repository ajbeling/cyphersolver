David Humphreys to Pickering, Madrid, 1799: the coded reports on Yrujo and Louisiana

Two coded passages from the American minister in Spain, left without a decipherment on the film, deciphered in large part with his lost code rebuilt from the State Department’s own decipherments of his other letters.

Daniel Bourdeau

At a glanceKey recovered from adjacent plaintext · partial (377 of 424 groups; 357 on the frozen table)

The letters
David Humphreys, United States minister to Spain, to Timothy Pickering, Secretary of State, from Madrid: the coded parts of despatch No. 188 (19 March 1799) and No. 214 (6 August 1799), and one coded sentence of April 1799.
Where
National Archives, RG 59, Despatches from U.S. Ministers to Spain, vol. 4, microfilm M31 roll 5, frames 0294, 0364 and 0414–0417, free in the National Archives Catalog.
The cipher
A one-part code of about 1,600 numbers for words, syllables, letters and punctuation in scrambled order: the code Jefferson made in 1785 for John Adams (Boyd’s “Code No. 8”), whose key is lost.
How it was deciphered
With a table of 686 groups rebuilt from the State Department clerks’ decipherments of Humphreys’s other despatches and from printed decoded texts, frozen, then tested on a held-out despatch: 129 of 135 groups right, shuffled tables at most 3%.
What it says
Humphreys’s belief, told to Urquijo, that France had never given up its design on Louisiana (“relinquished” is mostly guessed), with the Spanish minister Urquijo’s assurance that France {should} {never} get it while he held office; Humphreys’s supposition about why Spain delayed recalling its minister Yrujo; and in No. 188, probably Sweden’s [less inclined?] attitude to France.
Still open
36 code numbers with no value (37 with the April 1799 sentence), 12 groups with digits in the binding, and several named details (Basle, Saavedra, “voted”, Sweden, Staël) that rest on guesses. No person has checked the reading on the film.
Credit
The State Department’s clerks, whose decipherments, with the printed decoded texts, are the source of the table; the editors of the Papers of Thomas Jefferson, who decoded or verified the 1791 letters with a partial key of their own; F. L. Humphreys (1917) and the Madison Papers. Table, test and readings contributed by Andrew Beling, with AI agents.

01 Louisiana, August 1799

In his despatch No. 214 of 6 August 1799 Humphreys reports a conversation with Mariano Luis de Urquijo, named in the clear text. He wrote the frame of it in clear and the substance in code. Here is the coded part as it stands in the reading file, with its holes. Its first clause is Humphreys’s own belief, which he says he told Urquijo (“I had good reason to believe”, in clear), not a fact the despatch establishes. Words in braces are context values: {never} rests on the clerk’s value of the same number in another despatch; {re}, {ces}, {trea}, {ti}, {sion} and {should} are supported by another occurrence or by partly visible digits. Words in square brackets with a question mark are guesses from the sense; a number in brackets is a group left unread.

“that the design of {re}covering Louisiana had {never} been re[197][1355]ished … {ces}sion was [in?]serted in the [origin?]al project of the {trea}y of [Basl?]e … Truguet had re[22]d the negociation with saave[dra?] … he had [314]d against the ra{ti}fication of the treaty for want of that ces{sion} (which he said) would have been made but for trea[ch?]ery … so long as he {re}mained in offi[ce?] I might rest as[sure?]d France {should} {never} get possession of Louisiana.”

The page carries no image: crops of frame 0417 are left out of this pull request. The frames are free in the National Archives Catalog (M31 roll 5, frames 0414–0417).

The clear text around the code gives the speakers: Humphreys told Urquijo he was “not ignorant of the French intrigues on that subject”; he knew the sentiments of every French ambassador at Madrid since the peace; he could not “forbear mentioning that Guillemardet had declared to me” the grievance over the treaty, “as France had relinquished so much to Spain without an equivalent”; and Urquijo, who had negotiated the treaty with Iriarte, answered that it had no secret articles of importance, and then gave the assurance.

How strong each detail is. “Louisiana” is spelled letter by letter and occurs twice. “Relinquished” is three-quarters guessed (re-[197]-[1355]-ish-ed). “Basle” is weak: one group with no value, guessed, plus a final e; “Treaty” rests on reading 344 for a group read 244 at the line edge. “Truguet” is fairly strong (spelled Tru-gue-t; “Tru” rests on one entry). “Saavedra” is medium: its last syllable is a guess. “Treachery” is medium: tre-a-?-e-ry admits no other English word, but three of its pieces rest on single entries and the third is “child” in the table. What Guillemardet had done “against the ratification” is unread: “voted” is a guess and is not printed. Urquijo said Humphreys “might rest assured”; he did not swear.

02 Yrujo and the orders

The same despatch opens in clear with Spain’s “strange fluctuating conduct respecting the recal of Mr. de Yrujo”, the Spanish minister to the United States whose recall the Adams administration had asked for. The code goes on: Humphreys cannot account for it “upon any other [ground?] than by supposing he is con[ne?]ced with certain lea[d?][ing?] [men?] here in the profits of an exclusive [trade?] from the United States to the Spanish colonies and that his presence in America is necessary to com[ple?]te that operation”. This is Humphreys’s supposition, not a fact the despatch reports. “Ground”, “leading men” and “trade” are guesses (“trade” could be “commerce”), and so are single syllables of “connected” and “complete”; the rest is on the table. Later in the despatch he suggests that if the President should [646] to send him [away?], the reasons be explained in a letter to the King of Spain, and asks “to be honoured [with?] the orders of the President whether {to} remain at Lisbon or [proceed?] to the United States in case of my being obliged to leave this country”. Two short passages about a man who boasts of a flour licence for Havana and Caracas, and a letter “from Ur-u-i-[jo?]”, contain names that are not read.

03 No. 188, March 1799

Naval Documents Related to the Quasi-War between the United States and France prints No. 188 (vol. 2, 1935, p. 437) with three asterisks after a paragraph about the Swedish chargé d’affaires and Danish convoys. On the film the paragraph goes on “This Gentleman” and 56 code groups. In substance (a paraphrase): the government of [Sweden?] was now less inclined to humour France, and the Baron [935] sta[ël?] would probably soon leave Paris. Word for word the decode runs: “[ga?]ve me in confidence reason to believe that the government of [Sweden?] {now} less [inclin?]d to use management with that of France than [126] [?405] of that letter and that it is even probable the Baron [935] sta[ël?] would soon retire from Paris”. The gentleman is identified from the enclosures on the film (frames 0296–0297, letters of Carl Gustaf Adlerberg) as the Swedish chargé Adlerberg, which makes Sweden likely but not settled: the number for the country occurs nowhere else, and the clear sentence before it is about Denmark. The group between “Baron” and “Sta” is “rise” in the table, so “de Staël” is weak.

A third, short passage, in a despatch of April 1799 (frame 0364), reads in full: “I have seen a letter from the {Dutch} minister at {Paris} which reports that” (the two words in braces rest on a gloss found after the test and on a group with an uncertain last digit), followed in clear by the outcry against Scherer and Rewbell.

04 History and significance

Three things are kept apart here: what the despatches say (above); what the references establish, with the source; and our inference, marked as such. The one study of French policy read for this page is Frederick J. Turner, “The Policy of France toward the Mississippi Valley in the Period of Washington and Adams”, American Historical Review 10 (1905). The standard books (Arthur P. Whitaker, The Mississippi Question, 1934; E. Wilson Lyon, Louisiana in French Diplomacy, 1934; Alexander DeConde, This Affair of Louisiana, 1976) were not consulted.

Established. According to Turner, Barthélemy was instructed on 10 May 1795 to insist on Louisiana as the price of peace with Spain; at Basel in July 1795 Godoy made peace but kept Louisiana, giving up the Spanish part of Santo Domingo instead; France pressed again in the alliance talks of 1796, sent Perignon’s successor to offer a joint conquest of Portugal, and in 1797 offered the papal legations; in 1798 Talleyrand instructed Guillemardet, his minister at Madrid, to make Spain realize that it had been blind to its interests in putting the United States in possession of the Mississippi forts. Spain retroceded Louisiana to France by the secret treaty of San Ildefonso of 1 October 1800 (general reference, not from Turner). The Adams administration asked for Yrujo’s recall; under Jefferson the request was dropped and Yrujo stayed (Founders Online, editorial note in the Papers of Thomas Jefferson).

Our inference. Humphreys’s account of the treaty (if [Basl?]e is right) and of the French approaches agrees with Turner’s. Turner does not name Perignon’s successor; the despatch says Truguet had re[22]d the negotiation, and that Truguet was that successor is our inference. In August 1799, fourteen months before San Ildefonso, the American minister at Madrid told his government that France had never given up the design, with the French ambassador’s own grievance and the Spanish minister’s personal assurance to the contrary. Because it was in code and no decipherment is on the film, it is not known whether Pickering read it. The passage was not found in the literature consulted; whether Whitaker, Lyon or DeConde used it is not known. The commercial explanation of the Yrujo affair is a contemporary allegation; it was not found in the sources read. (Who signed the treaty for Spain was not checked here.)

05 The code and its table

Humphreys had been secretary of the American commission in Paris and used the code Jefferson made in 1785 for his correspondence with John Adams: Julian Boyd’s “Code No. 8”, Ralph Weber’s unlocated code in which 994 means “the”. He used it from Madrid in 1791 and as minister to Spain in 1797–1801. Its original key is lost. The editors of the Papers of Thomas Jefferson decoded his letters of January 1791 “employing Code No. 8”, later describing it as “partially reconstructed”; that working key is unpublished and was not used here. F. L. Humphreys’s Life and Times of David Humphreys (1917) prints the 1791 letters decoded by a “Dr. Lowndes”, and the Madison Papers print the clerk Jacob Wagner’s decodings of 1801.

The State Department’s clerks wrote the plain words over the code on many of Humphreys’s despatches. Group by group, those decipherments and the printed decoded texts give 3,368 pairs, each tied to roll, frame and line and labelled by kind: the clerk’s word over one group, the clerk’s words over several groups split here, or a printed edition. From them a table of 686 groups was built and frozen (its sha256 is in the folder). A test written down first then scored it on despatch No. 262 of 24 February 1801, kept out of the build: 129 of 135 groups decode to the clerk’s element (precision 99.2%); 200 tables with the same values shuffled reach 3.0% at most. A stricter count by a second agent, on groups that carry one whole word, gives 74 of 78. Values the table lacks were filled from the sense only in a separate file, marked by how they are supported, and every word of the readings is marked as table value, context value or guess. The repository’s letter-model validation is underdetermined: with about two tokens per number it cannot tell this reading from a key fitted to shuffled text, so the held-out test is the evidence.

06 How it was done

  1. Every frame of M31 rolls 4–6 and M43 rolls 2–3 was downloaded from the National Archives Catalog and searched with OCR and the catalog’s own text layer for code; 16 despatches in this code were found and listed with roll and frame.
  2. The crib pages (the 1791 letters, the interlined despatches of 1800–01, the postscript of 30 March 1801) were transcribed and aligned with the clerks’ words and the printed texts; six of them were given a second pass after the test (1,533 of 1,541 groups agree).
  3. The table was frozen and the held-out test run as written.
  4. The coded parts of Nos. 188 and 214 were transcribed twice, the second time from the end backwards; the two passes agree. A second AI agent transcribed No. 188 and passages a and e of No. 214 itself, checked passages f–j group by group on the image, confirmed 40 of 40 sampled pairs, reran the test and set the wording of every content claim; one misread group (1393, “should”) was corrected.
  5. Rolls 5–7 were searched for second copies of both despatches (none), and the printed sources for the coded text (none found).

07 What remains uncertain

  • Twelve groups of No. 214 have digits hidden in the binding on frames 0415 and 0417; there is no second copy on the film, and the copies sent by other ships are not on it.
  • 36 code numbers in the two despatches have no value in the table (37 with group 1290 of the April 1799 sentence) (among them 197, 1355, 22, 314, 1519 in the Louisiana passages; 1097, 864, 126 in No. 188; 713, 983, 426 in names). The context values given to some of them are not confirmed by a second occurrence.
  • Whether the State Department deciphered the copies of Nos. 188 and 214 it received first is not established.
  • Three rolls (M31 roll 3, the rest of roll 7, M43 roll 4) were searched through the catalog text layer only. Humphreys’s own papers at Yale were not checked for clear drafts.
  • Nothing has been checked by a person: the transcriptions, the table, the test and the readings are AI agents’ work, checked by a second AI agent.
